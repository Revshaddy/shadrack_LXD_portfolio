How I design

Good training works like a well-built machine. Every part has a job, and you can check that it’s doing it.

A clear purpose

I start with the job people need to do and the gap in the way, so every activity ties back to a real performance goal.

Tested parts

I prototype early and try each piece with real learners, the same way I taught students to test a robot before competition day.

Results you can measure

I plan how we’ll know it worked before I build anything, from quiz data to what changes on the job.

Case study: technical skills program

Robotec 2022: from first moves to an autonomous factory robot

A holiday robotics camp built on LEGO® Education’s MINDSTORMS EV3 “Robot Trainer” unit. Learners start with a basic two-motor robot and, one capability at a time, program it to sense obstacles, carry loads, follow lines and navigate by gyro, then combine everything in a factory-floor mission.

My role
Organizer, designer, lead facilitator, instructor trainer
Learners
60+ across multiple cohorts
Format
Hands-on holiday camp, teams of 2–3 per kit
I designed
Sequence, session routine, scenario, quick card, rubric

The design problem

  • Beginners with very different starting points.Some learners had never programmed; others had built robots before. The program had to keep both groups moving.
  • A short, intensive window.A holiday camp has no homework time, so every session had to end with something working.
  • A real integrated goal.I wanted learners to finish by solving an open-ended mission on their own, not just by completing worksheets.
  • Several instructors, one standard.Other instructors ran sessions too, so the routine had to be easy to teach and repeat.

One new capability per session, building to a capstone

I chose and ordered seven lessons so each one adds exactly one new sensor or mechanism and reuses everything before it. By Lesson 6 nothing is new; the challenge is combining skills. This is the same structure I use for onboarding people to new equipment: introduce one function, practice it, then integrate it into the real task.

  1. Lesson 1Moves and turnsDrive motors B C: rotations, degrees, turn types, pseudocode. Challenge: drive exactly 84 cm.
  2. Lesson 2Objects and obstaclesUltrasonic sensor 4: stop before an object, beep faster as it gets closer.
  3. Lesson 3Grab and releaseMedium motor arm A: grab a load, move it, release it. Combines with Lesson 2.
  4. Lesson 4Colors and linesColor sensor 3: calibrate, detect a line, follow it smoothly.
  5. Lesson 5Angles and patternsGyro sensor 2: accurate turns when wheels slip; reusable My Blocks to organize code.
  6. Lesson 6, capstoneThe factory robotNo new hardware. Teams design extensions, deliver a load to a drop-off zone, and follow a line to the charging station.
  7. Lesson 7, transferThe guided missionA competition-style mission on a FIRST LEGO League field: break the problem down and solve it with skills from the whole camp.

The session routine I trained instructors to run

Every lesson follows the same five beats, so learners always know what comes next and any instructor can pick up a session.

  1. HookA real-world question: “What robots do you know that detect objects?”
  2. ObserveRun a ready-made program and describe what the robot does.
  3. ModifyRebuild the behavior from the same blocks.
  4. ChallengeAn open task with a measurable target, like 84 cm.
  5. Reflect“What did you do well? What could you do better?”

The routine moves learners from guided practice to independence inside each session, and the capstone repeats that move across the whole camp.

A branching scenario I designed from the most common Lesson 4 fault. It practices diagnosis, not just steps: what would you check first?

Facilitator quick card: common faults by lesson

A one-page reference so every instructor can spot and coach the same problems the same way.

LessonSymptomCoach them to check
1 MovesRobot drives in a curve or misses 84 cmBoth wheels spin freely; motors in B and C; measure, then adjust rotations, not guess
2 ObstaclesRobot hits the objectSensor in port 4, facing forward; stopping distance, not speed, is the first fix
3 GrabArm misses or drops the loadArm motor in A; slow the arm speed; stop at the right distance first

How the program is evaluated

Reaction

The reflection prompt that closes every lesson.

Learning

Each lesson’s measurable challenge, such as an exact distance or a shape pattern.

Transfer

The factory robot and guided mission, where teams solve problems with no step-by-step instructions.

Why this matters for workplace training

The same design carries over to onboarding people onto equipment and systems. At L’Oréal USA and Cardinal Health I introduce one procedure at a time, check it before moving on, give staff a quick reference for common faults, and finish with the integrated real task. Robotec is the example; the method is the work.
